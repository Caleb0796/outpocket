**TRIED.** Generate `artifacts/tools.export.json` by driving a real ERP into each of the six
canonical states and reading the surface out of the real compiler rather than transcribing a

**HAPPENED.** Four clauses green. Two things I did not expect. First, the export's state ids
and the compiler's state ids are different namespaces that **collide numerically at 2 and 3**
— export `S2-emp-draft-clean` is internal S3, export `S3-emp-draft-dirty` is internal S2.
Second, the frozen schema's worked example handed me three numbers for free: `schema_bytes`
33 and `name_bytes` 17 reproduced exactly and confirmed the whole OCF-1 pipeline before I had
written a line of the generator (they also told me something undocumented — name bytes are
inside `total_bytes` but are NOT an accounting field, 230+33+17=280). The third,
`description_bytes` 230, diverged from our 266 and cost about fifteen minutes of git
archaeology before it resolved: the example was measured 2026-08-28 against the pre-port
countinghouse spike, whose header lists only five states. Nothing had drifted inside
outpocket — the description is byte-identical to our first commit.

**CHANGED.** Two design decisions moved mid-node. `app_commit` started as
`git rev-parse HEAD` and became a scoped source set after I found `erp/RISK.md` §5's E5 drift
guard: had it tracked HEAD, committing the artifact would move HEAD and every later
generation would differ, so **the guard would false-alarm forever and get switched off**. And
my own leak test started as a raw substring sweep, which reported "the export leaks session"
— because `get_session_scope` is a legitimate tool name and `session` is on
`x-forbiddenKeys`. Those are KEYS, not text. The generator had walked keys from the start;
the test had not. **I fixed the test to match the generator, not the other way round.**

**EARLIER.** What I would tell myself an hour before: *you are about to read `compile.js` and
see `MEMBERSHIP.S2 = 12` dirty and `S3 = 13` clean, and you will not notice that the export's
S2 is the clean one.* I didn't. My note at that moment was "T1 is done, and T6 too since S5
has `get_report`" — **the crossover was in front of me and I walked past it.** What caught
it, roughly twenty minutes later, was the frozen schema's `x-requiredStates`: a second,
redundant, numeric statement of the same six facts, which contradicted the mapping I had not
yet written but would have. The charter had even told me "13 is the clean draft, 12 is the
dirty draft" and I still did not connect a fact about COUNTS to a hazard about IDS. **Being
told the counts is not being told the namespaces collide.** The natural implementation is a
zip of two six-element lists that are both honestly "in order," and nothing in the corpus
ever puts the two id namespaces adjacent — which is why the guard had to go into code as an
explicit `internal` per state with a landing assertion, and not into a comment. So: the
comfortable lesson would be "I spotted the dangerous one." **The true one is that a redundant
restatement of the same numbers in a second frozen document did the work, and I should go
LOOKING for that second statement at the start of a node rather than be grateful when it
happens to exist.**
